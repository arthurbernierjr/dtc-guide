# Grit: The Long Return

> *The summoning circle gives you a second life, a borrowed coat, and a status screen. It does not give you the person you hoped you would become. That part, apparently, is still your job.*

## 01 — The skill the summoning forgot

The last thing you remember about your old world is a browser with nineteen tabs open. One promises to teach you programming in a weekend. Another explains why the language in the first tab is already dead. Your coffee has developed a skin. Somewhere behind the windows, a project called final-final-actually-final has been waiting for you to open it again.

Then your apartment folds inward like a closing book.

You land face-first on cold stone.

A blue moon hangs above a ruined observatory. A second moon, smaller and copper-colored, looks as though somebody has taken a bite out of it. Around your wrist, green letters assemble themselves in the air.

**SUMMONING COMPLETE. CLASS: RUNEWRIGHT. LEVEL: 1.**

You wait for the rest. A legendary weapon. Infinite mana. The ability to understand every programming language because a goddess accidentally gave you administrator privileges.

**STARTING EQUIPMENT: ONE COAT. ONE STYLUS.**

The coat has a hole in it.

“That belongs to the guild,” says someone behind you. “Try not to die in it. Blood is expensive to get out.”

The woman on the observatory steps carries a lantern made from a jam jar. Her name is Iona. She maintains the town's wards, teaches its apprentices, and apparently handles interdimensional arrivals when the night porter is sick. She offers you a hand, then a heel of bread wrapped in paper.

“Can I go home?” you ask.

“We don't know how to reopen the crossing yet.”

“Am I supposed to save the world?”

“Eventually, perhaps. Tomorrow the west market needs a sign.”

This is an insulting first quest. You have been transported across the fabric of reality to do signage.

But the next morning, when you scratch three symbols into a slate and the words FRESH BREAD lift from its surface in warm gold, something catches in your chest. The baker laughs. You laugh. You change the letters to FRESH BREAD, PROBABLY, and get hit with a towel.

Your status screen chimes. Level two.

By sunset you have lit a doorway, repaired a kettle's heating rune, and made a small green frog appear above your palm. You go to sleep certain that this world has finally revealed who you really are.

Three weeks later, you cannot make a gate open for the right person.

Your status screen has stopped chiming.

The frog still works. You cast it sometimes when nobody is watching, just to remember what being good at something felt like.

## 02 — The contract beneath the excitement

The troublesome gate belongs to the lower gardens. It should admit anyone carrying a gardener's seal and stay closed for everyone else. Your version admits gardeners on Tuesdays, rejects them when it rains, and once opened for a wheelbarrow. You have begun to hate that wheelbarrow personally.

Across the workshop, Tavi finishes the same exercise before breakfast. Tavi grew up repairing fishing nets and seems to understand every system by looking at the spaces between its parts. Their gate opens, closes, and gives a polite little bow.

You decide that being summoned was probably a clerical error.

Iona finds you packing the borrowed coat.

“Where are you going?”

“The beast-tamers are accepting apprentices.”

“Do you want to work with animals?”

“I want to be good at something.”

She pulls up a stool. Outside, rain taps the windows with the patience of a creditor.

“Those are different wants.”

You explain the evidence: your slow progress, Tavi's speed, the wheelbarrow. Iona listens without rescuing you from the embarrassment of saying it aloud. Then she asks what you liked about the baker's sign.

You almost say the level-up. Instead, you remember the baker coming outside to see his own shop glowing in the dawn. His hands were white with flour. He kept stepping backward into the street to look at it again.

“I made something useful,” you say. “Something that wasn't there before.”

“Would you like to become someone who can keep doing that?”

Yes. Even now, with the gate broken and your pride worse, yes.

That is where your understanding of grit begins. Angela Duckworth describes grit as **passion and perseverance for long-term goals**. Both parts matter. Perseverance is the willingness to keep working through difficulty. Passion gives that work a direction you continue to care about. Without direction, you can become very determined at avoiding the thing that matters.

The passion in this definition does not require you to wake up thrilled every morning. You were thrilled by the frog. You are interested in becoming a builder. Those feelings can overlap, but they are not identical. One is an exciting afternoon. The other can organize years of choices.

Iona gives you a blank contract. There is no oath to suffer forever, no promise that you will never change your mind. The term is six weeks from your arrival, with a review at the end. You are already halfway through.

You write: **I want to build things people can rely on.**

Below it: **For the rest of this term, I will learn the warding fundamentals, ask for feedback, and finish one useful repair.**

“And if runewriting turns out to be wrong for me?”

“Then we'll discuss what you learned. Tonight you're frightened. That's real. It doesn't have to make every decision.”

You unpack the coat.

## 03 — The country between level-ups

The next day, Iona takes you beyond the northern wall. The road climbs through pine trees, bends sharply, then opens onto a broad shelf of stone. For a long distance it looks almost flat. Beyond it, another ridge rises into cloud.

“This,” she says, “is the bit they cut out of the ballads.”

You know the ballads. A summoned stranger discovers a hidden power. A training sequence follows: snow, sweat, dramatic music, perhaps a conveniently placed waterfall. By the final chorus, the stranger can split a mountain.

Nobody sings about the morning when the stranger does the same exercise as yesterday and remains the same height.

George Leonard's *Mastery* describes a path that makes room for exactly this experience. Improvement often includes stretches where visible performance seems to flatten. Practice continues, but the exciting evidence becomes less frequent. A breakthrough may arrive, followed by another stretch of ordinary work. The plateau is part of the landscape.

You sit beside Iona and draw your expected progress in the dust: a heroic diagonal line.

She draws a more awkward route, with long shelves and uneven rises.

“Does staying on a shelf guarantee the next climb?” you ask.

“No. You can spend a year rehearsing the same mistake. That's why we look at what you're practicing.”

That distinction matters. Loving the plateau does not mean declaring every stalled method sacred. It means you stop treating the absence of a dramatic reward as proof that the whole journey is worthless. You can accept the season you are in while adjusting the work you do there.

On the walk home, she asks what has changed since your first gate attempt.

You can recognize the seal-reading symbol now. You know which mark holds the gate's current state. Yesterday you found a mismatched bracket without asking. The gate still fails, but the failure is no longer one enormous, nameless darkness.

These are smaller measures than a level-up. They are also evidence.

Back at the workshop, you divide a page in your journal. On one side: what the ward should do. On the other: what you can now explain, test, or repair without help. The first side is discouraging. The second has grown.

For the first time in days, you leave the workshop without casting the frog for reassurance.

Then you cast it anyway, because it is a very good frog.

## 04 — Three doors out of the workshop

There are three doors in the guild's training hall. Only one actually leads outside, but Iona says apprentices manage to escape through all of them.

The first opens onto the equipment market. A man there sells spellbooks bound in iridescent leather. Each promises to make the previous spellbook unnecessary. You have already bought two, including one whose title contains the word ultimate twice.

This is the escape Leonard calls the **Dabbler**: the beginning feels wonderful, progress slows, and a fresh beginning offers relief. Exploring different interests is useful. The trap begins when every encounter with difficulty becomes evidence that you should shop for a new identity.

You recognize this door. It has nineteen browser tabs behind it.

The second door opens onto the courtyard, where an apprentice named Renn practices after everyone else has gone home. When his results worsen, he adds more hours. He skips dinner. He redraws the same ward with increasingly angry strokes until his hand shakes and the mistakes multiply.

Leonard calls this pattern the **Obsessive**. It tries to force progress into an uninterrupted climb. The apprentice interprets a plateau as an insult and answers it with punishment. You admire Renn until the morning he falls asleep against a brazier and burns a hole in his sleeve.

The third door leads to a comfortable room with excellent biscuits. Here an older apprentice shows you a gate charm that works beautifully for exactly one seal. He uses it for everything, then explains away each situation it cannot handle.

This is Leonard's **Hacker**: someone who reaches a workable level and settles there, avoiding the practice that would reveal and stretch their limits. His label here describes a learning habit; it is not a judgment about programmers who call themselves hackers, and it has nothing to do with breaking into systems.

None of these people is a permanent species. You can visit all three rooms before lunch. You can buy a new course, punish yourself for being behind, and then hide inside an exercise you already know, all while telling yourself you spent the day learning.

Iona does not confiscate the spellbooks, scold Renn into greatness, or outlaw biscuits. She asks each of you to choose a specific skill that needs work and a practice session you can actually sustain.

Your task is embarrassingly small: make a training slate distinguish a valid seal from an invalid one, without connecting it to a gate.

No moving machinery. No decorative flame. No bow.

You put the ultimate spellbooks away. Tavi brings you a biscuit. You begin.

## 05 — Four turns of the training circle

Iona draws a circle on the bench and divides it into four sections. At the center she places the slate, three seals, and your expensive stylus.

“Today,” she says, “we find out where understanding stops.”

The first section is **explanation**. She tells you that the slate receives a seal, checks a condition, and produces a result. Your mistake has been checking the paint on the seal rather than its guild mark. Rain changes the paint. Tuesdays were a coincidence you invented from too little evidence.

You say the idea back: “I need to check the credential itself, not something that happens to look like it.”

She asks what should happen when there is no seal at all. You hesitate. Good. The explanation has exposed an assumption before a real gate has to suffer for it.

The second section is **demonstration**. Iona draws the ward slowly. Before each mark she explains what she expects it to change. Then she tests a valid seal, an invalid seal, and an empty hand. She deliberately damages one mark so you can watch how she locates the error.

This is the part you usually fast-forward. You want to see the finished spell. Watching someone investigate a failure feels less glamorous than watching them produce a perfect result. Today you notice that investigation is most of the skill you were missing.

The third section is **imitation**. You copy her ward onto your own slate. Your first copy fails because two symbols that look alike have different meanings. You repair them. Then you explain each mark while pointing to it. When you cannot explain one, you return to that part of the demonstration.

A working copy is useful evidence: you can follow a known route. It is not yet evidence that you can choose the route yourself.

Iona removes her slate.

The fourth section is **independent practice**. This time the slate must recognize a healer's seal for the infirmary supply room. Same underlying idea, different purpose and different examples. You are allowed to consult your notes. You are required to decide which parts apply.

You freeze.

There it is: the exact distance between recognizing a solution and producing one.

Iona does not interpret the silence as a verdict. She asks which decision you can make first. You choose the input. Then the condition. Then the three cases that will tell you whether it works. When you get stuck, you ask a precise question about the mark you do not understand.

By late afternoon, the infirmary slate recognizes the right seal. You draw a fresh version without the finished one beside you. It is uglier. It works.

**Explain. Demonstrate. Imitate. Practice.** This is the teaching loop used in this guide, and it gives you a way to diagnose your learning. If you cannot explain an idea, revisit the explanation. If you understand the words but cannot see the sequence, watch a demonstration. If you have never made the steps work, imitate them. If you can only succeed beside the original, practice a variation that forces you to choose.

You can move backward around the circle. You can ask for help inside any section. The aim is to become progressively more responsible for the decisions.

On your wrist, the status screen remains quiet.

On the table, something you understand answers something you asked.

## 06 — The ledger that counts effort twice

Tavi's gate has acquired a second feature. It now apologizes when it closes too quickly.

You try to be pleased for them. What comes out of your mouth is, “Must be nice.”

Tavi sets down their stylus. “What?”

“Having it come naturally.”

They turn their notebook toward you. The first page contains a drawing of a fishing net. Behind it are years of knots, repairs, diagrams, and little experiments with tension. Tavi has been thinking about inputs, connections, and failures since childhood. This does not make their talent imaginary. It makes your comparison incomplete.

Iona draws two relationships in the guild ledger. Duckworth's model connects talent and effort to skill, then skill and effort to achievement. Effort appears in both relationships: you invest it while developing an ability, and again when you use that ability to make something happen.

Learning to read a seal builds skill. Applying that skill to a dependable garden gate produces a useful result. A shelf full of tutorials might help the first process, but it cannot substitute for doing either one.

These relationships are a model, not a literal character-stat calculator. You cannot enter six hours and demand six units of success. Teachers, time, equipment, health, opportunity, and luck affect what becomes possible. Iona's workshop matters. So does having a warm room where you can make mistakes without losing your livelihood.

You had been using Tavi's speed to answer a question it could not answer: whether your own effort was worth making.

That evening you change the ledger you keep for yourself. Instead of recording only minutes at the bench, you record the target of the session, what you tried, what happened, and what you will adjust. Two hours of redrawing a comfortable rune no longer disguise themselves as the same work as twenty attentive minutes on a weakness.

Your first entry reads: *Target: distinguish a missing seal from an invalid seal. Result: I treated them as the same case. Next: test empty input before checking the mark.*

It is a deeply unromantic sentence. Nobody would embroider it on a royal banner.

But tomorrow, when you sit down, you will know where to begin.

You apologize to Tavi. They accept, then ask for help making the gate's apology less sarcastic. Its current version says, YOUR REFLEXES HAVE BEEN NOTED.

Apparently, everyone still has something to practice.

## 07 — The lantern under the practice

At the start of your fifth week, Iona takes you to the lower gardens. Past the gate are vegetable beds, a washhouse, and a stone channel that carries water down from the hills. You have been thinking of the place as a test environment with cabbages.

Then you meet the people who use it.

Mara tends the night vegetables, pale leaves that unfurl under the copper moon. Her father walks slowly and cannot keep leaning over the gate's manual latch. A boy named Pell carries deliveries between the beds and the market. He wants the gate to stay open long enough for his cart, which is the wheelbarrow you have been blaming for your problems.

“It has a name,” Pell says. “Captain.”

You apologize to Captain as well.

The garden ward is one piece of a larger system. When heavy rain comes, channel gates divert water away from the town. The old controls are deteriorating. Experienced wardens are repairing the main junctions; apprentices are rebuilding small, supervised parts.

Suddenly your exercise has neighbors.

Duckworth identifies four assets that help grit develop: **interest, practice, purpose, and hope**. You can feel the differences now. Interest was the strange pleasure of making the bread sign glow. Practice is the work of targeting a weakness, trying carefully, getting feedback, and adjusting. Purpose enters when you see whose life your growing ability might help. Hope is the belief that your actions can contribute to improvement, even when the outcome is uncertain.

They support each other, but they are not interchangeable. A moving reason to build the gate cannot teach you the missing rune. Repeating the rune cannot decide whether this work matters to you. Telling yourself everything will work out cannot replace an experiment that changes what you know.

That afternoon, Pell asks if the gate will announce his name when he arrives.

“Eventually,” you say.

Iona raises an eyebrow.

“After it opens reliably,” you add.

You begin to understand what purpose asks of you. It can make the work more meaningful, but it also makes your choices less theatrical. The people in the gardens need a latch that behaves predictably. They do not need proof that you are the most gifted person ever summoned.

On the way back, Iona asks what you imagine when you picture becoming a runewright.

The old picture is still there: robes, applause, impossible power. Beside it, a new picture has appeared. Mara's father walks through a gate without struggling. Pell pushes Captain through without being trapped halfway.

You can see what you want to make. Now you can choose the next small task that serves it.

## 08 — The evening you do not return

The trouble with discovering a purpose is that you can turn it into another weapon against yourself.

For three nights you stay late. There is always one more test. One more improvement. Somewhere, you decide that caring about the gardens means never leaving the workshop while anything remains unfinished.

On the fourth evening, you miss practice entirely.

You eat cold bread in your rented room and stare at the wall. The stylus lies beside the bed. Picking it up feels like lifting a door. By morning, your mind has written an elaborate case against you: unreliable, lazy, another abandoned beginning.

Iona knocks at noon.

“I'm breaking the contract,” you tell her.

She looks at your unwashed cup, your untouched breakfast, the practice ledger filled past midnight.

“Your contract includes a person,” she says. “We should account for them.”

Leonard writes about resistance to change, including the pull of established habits. A new practice has to find a place in an existing life. Expecting that life to reorganize itself without friction leaves you surprised by every obstacle. Support, a regular rhythm, and adjustments make the practice more workable.

You and Iona review your week. You still owe kitchen shifts in exchange for rent. You need sleep. You have been spending the first half of each practice session deciding what to practice, then extending the second half because you feel behind.

Together you make the next session smaller and clearer. After tomorrow's kitchen shift, you will test one behavior for twenty minutes. Before stopping, you will write the next question in the ledger. Tavi will meet you twice a week to compare results. Rest belongs in the plan. So does a way to resume after interruption.

There is a difference between discomfort you can learn through and a routine that keeps damaging your ability to learn. Adjusting the routine is part of caring for the long-term goal. Grit cannot mean using the future as an excuse to destroy every present day.

The next afternoon, you arrive late. Your sleeve smells of onions. You do not feel transformed or especially courageous.

You open the ledger. You test the missing-seal case. You find a mistake, fix it, and write down what changed. Then you leave at the time you agreed.

No message declares your streak restored. The world makes no announcement.

Tavi moves a chair aside as you pass. “See you Thursday?”

“Thursday,” you say.

Your return has a date instead of a speech.

## 09 — A beginner in front of witnesses

The public review is worse than the gate.

Six apprentices gather around your slate. Iona asks you to explain why the ward should stay closed when it cannot read a seal. You start confidently, lose track of your explanation halfway through, and point at a mark whose name you have forgotten.

There is a silence large enough to furnish.

Your first instinct is to fill it with complicated words. Your second is to say the system is experimental. Both would protect your image for perhaps thirty seconds and leave the confusion intact.

“I don't understand that part well enough yet,” you say.

Tavi slides you a piece of paper. “Draw what happens first.”

Leonard includes surrender in the path of mastery: a willingness to be a learner, to receive instruction, and to release the need to look competent at every moment. In a good learning environment, this is not surrendering your judgment to someone who humiliates you. It is letting a useful correction matter more than the performance of already knowing.

Iona's standards remain high. She does not pretend the gate is ready. She also does not turn your mistake into entertainment. She asks one question at a time and waits while you work through the answer.

You draw the seal arriving. Then the check. Then the decision. At the failure branch, your diagram simply stops.

“There,” you say.

There is the gap. Not your entire character. Not a cosmic mistake in the summoning. One missing decision in one system, visible enough to work on.

You leave the review with a correction, two test cases, and much less mysterious fear. The criticism hurt, but it had a shape and a use. That is what you should seek when you ask for feedback on your own code: something specific enough to guide another attempt, offered in a way that leaves room for learning.

Later, Renn comes back to the workshop. His sleeve has been patched. He asks whether he can join your shorter practice sessions.

You make room.

The guild begins to feel less like a leaderboard and more like a place where people return. You watch Tavi ask an elementary question. You watch Iona consult a manual. The most experienced person in the room does not crumble when the answer lives on a page instead of in her head.

That night, you remove LEGENDARY from the top of your practice ledger. You had written it during the first week.

You leave your name.

## 10 — The gate that would not open

The storm arrives two days before the end of your term.

By dusk, water is running down the steps of the guild hall. By nightfall, the channel above the lower gardens is carrying branches. The wardens activate the town's diversion plan. Iona sends you, Tavi, and Renn to the garden junction with a maintenance lantern and strict instructions: inspect the local ward, report what you find, and leave the main controls to the experienced crew.

Your gate has passed its supervised tests. You should feel proud.

Instead, when Mara presses her seal to the post, the gate stays shut.

The old feeling returns instantly. Three weeks vanish. You are back in the workshop, stupid before a wheelbarrow. Somewhere inside your skull, a voice offers the familiar verdict: *You were never going to be good at this.*

Tavi says your name.

Rain crawls down the inside of your collar. The maintenance lantern shakes in your hand. You want a hidden power to awaken. You want the story to reward you for getting this far.

Nothing awakens.

So you ask Mara to show you exactly what happened.

Her seal is valid. The ward reads it correctly. You follow the next step and discover that the gate has entered its protective closed state because a separate water sensor is reporting a dangerous level. That behavior is intentional. Your first impulse was to force it open; your training lets you pause long enough to see why that would be a bad idea.

You signal Iona with the finding. The main crew confirms that the sensor is correct: debris is blocking the downstream channel. Opening the wrong route would send more water toward the washhouse.

Your beautiful ward cannot solve that problem.

The realization stings. Then it frees you to notice the actual task. Your commitment was to help the gardens function safely. It was never a sacred vow to make your favorite spell the answer to every situation.

Under the wardens' direction, Renn and a maintenance crew clear the obstruction from the safe access point. Tavi checks the alternate route. You stay with Mara and the local controls, reporting the water readings and keeping people away from the locked passage. Pell takes Captain up the hill with the other deliveries.

For nearly an hour, your most useful contribution is accurate observation.

When the main crew gives the signal, you test the ward again. The sensor is back within its permitted range. Mara's seal registers. The gate opens.

It is not a spectacular opening. No column of light splits the sky. A hinge complains. Mud slides beneath the frame.

Mara gets through. Her father follows. Behind them, water moves into the cleared channel, away from the washhouse.

You stand in the rain while the system does the ordinary thing it was built to do.

You could mistake this ending for a lesson about trying the same thing until the universe gives in. Look closer. You changed your understanding, asked for help, respected a limit, and worked with other people. You stopped a bad tactic without abandoning the purpose behind it. The return was intelligent because it remained open to evidence.

By the time the storm passes, you are too tired to check your level.

## 11 — Morning after the victory

The morning after saving part of the town, you expect at least a small ceremony.

Iona hands you a broom.

“The workshop flooded.”

There are songs that end at exactly the right moment to avoid this part. The monster falls, the crowd cheers, the credits roll before anyone has to dry the notebooks. Your life, inconveniently, continues after the scene that would have made a satisfying ending.

You sweep. Renn hangs damp gloves over a chair. Tavi rewrites the storm notes so the next crew will understand them. Someone brings bread. The baker has added DEFINITELY beneath FRESH BREAD on his sign.

At the term review, Iona asks whether you still want to be a runewright.

You think before answering. You know more about the work now: its patient details, its mistakes, the dependence on people you once hoped to impress without needing. You also know the small delight of tracing a failure to its cause, and the larger satisfaction of making something useful enough to become unremarkable.

“Yes,” you say. “I want another term.”

You set a new goal: learn to design and test a small ward from a written request. The next exercise is harder than the gate. On the first attempt, you fail in a way you have never seen before.

For several seconds, you are furious.

Then you laugh, which does not fix the ward but makes enough room to start looking at it.

This is Leonard's practice as a noun: a recurring place in your life where you do the work of becoming more capable. A practice can hold ambition without demanding a triumph from every session. You can enjoy the grain of the work itself, even while caring about the things it lets you build.

The pleasure is quiet. A clearer question. A cleaner explanation. A mistake you recognize sooner. The moment your hands begin a familiar task and your attention settles inside it. There will still be tedious days. You are learning that a worthwhile life contains them without being reduced to them.

The crossing home remains unsolved. You have begun helping Iona study its old diagrams, one small piece at a time. You no longer imagine that a second world can spare you the work you avoided in the first. But you have found a way to do that work with people, and a reason to care about where it leads.

Your wrist lights up while you are washing the cups.

**LEVEL INCREASED.**

You grin so widely that Renn asks what happened.

You show him. Of course you show him. Learning to value the path does not require pretending the milestones feel bad.

Then you finish washing the cups.

## 12 — Your quest beyond the page

You are back at your own screen now. The moons, the ward, and Captain belong to the story. The next decision belongs to you.

Choose one small project you care about: a readable page for someone you know, a quest list you can actually use, a form that handles a mistake kindly. If you are already on [the curriculum path](/path/), use the lesson you are working through. You do not need another grand beginning to practice returning.

Write a sentence naming the capability you want to develop and why it matters. “I want to build pages people can navigate on a phone” gives your work a direction. Then choose a short period of practice that fits your real commitments and a date when you will review what you learned. This is an experiment you can revise, not a lifelong sentence handed down by your least confident afternoon.

For your next session, identify the turn of the learning circle you need. Get an explanation you can restate. Watch a demonstration closely enough to understand its decisions. Make a working copy. Then change the problem enough that you have to choose a solution. If your copy works but your variation does not, you have located the next place to learn.

Pick one weakness and a way to observe it. Perhaps your layout breaks on a narrow screen. Perhaps your function handles a normal input but fails on an empty one. Predict what should happen, try it, compare the result, and adjust. Ask for feedback with the smallest example that shows your confusion. A question someone can answer is part of the work.

Before you stop, leave three lines in your journal: **what I tried; what I learned; what I will try next**. Record evidence of growing independence as well as finished features. A calendar full of effort tells one story. A record of decisions you can now make tells another useful one.

Arrange your return. Give it a time, a place, and a size you can manage. Include breaks. If life interrupts, resume from the next useful step instead of assigning yourself a punishment backlog. If a method keeps failing, seek a different explanation, more focused feedback, or a smaller problem. If the goal no longer deserves your commitment, review it honestly. Choosing matters too.

You may feel the old impulse to close this page, find a more exciting tutorial, and imagine the person that tutorial will make you. Stay for one small action first. Open the project. Read the error. Write the question you have been avoiding.

Somewhere in the other world, an apprentice sits down at a scarred wooden bench. The gate outside opens for the morning gardeners. A little green frog watches from the corner of the desk.

There is no audience.

You pick up the stylus.

## The books behind the quest

This is original teaching fiction, informed by Brian Johnson's **Philosopher's Notes on Angela Duckworth's *Grit: The Power of Passion and Perseverance*** and **George Leonard's *Mastery: The Keys to Success and Long-Term Fulfillment***. The grit note was read in the *Top 10 + One Philosopher's Notes* collection; the mastery note was read as a separate note. The characters and events are invented, and the dialogue is not quoted from either author.

From Duckworth: long-term passion and perseverance; effort in both developing and using skill; interest, practice, purpose, and hope; and the value of a supportive culture with high standards. Her [public introduction to *Grit*](https://angeladuckworth.com/grit-book/) offers a route to the book. From Leonard: the plateau; the Dabbler, Obsessive, and Hacker patterns; regular practice as part of a life; willingness to learn; intention; and support through resistance to change.

The four-turn Explain, Demonstrate, Imitate, Practice loop and the learner's quest are this guide's teaching structure. They are used here to turn those ideas into actions you can try. Neither grit nor mastery is a guarantee of success, a measure of someone's worth, or a reason to ignore the circumstances and support a learner needs.
