# Venture capital

A founder and an investor can both admire a product while wanting different futures for it. The founder may want a durable business serving a particular community. A venture fund may need a return large enough to matter across a portfolio where other investments fail.

That difference explains why a useful, profitable business is not automatically a venture-scale business. It also explains why fundraising conversations ask about market size, growth, ownership, and eventual liquidity rather than only whether people like the software.

## Translate the money into a changed company

An investment does more than extend the bank balance. It changes who owns the company and can introduce governance rights, reporting responsibilities, and expectations about pace. The amount raised is therefore an incomplete description of the deal.

Imagine two plans for the same guild-management product. One serves fifty clubs and pays its maintainer. The other aims to become infrastructure for a much larger market. Their staffing, distribution, and financing needs may be very different. Neither becomes the correct plan merely because it sounds more impressive.

[Unit 11's two roads](/path/11-1-rallying-the-villagers/) make that choice explicit. Start with the outcome you want to build toward, then examine the financing that supports it. A funding decision should help the company become more itself, rather than quietly committing it to a different destination.
