# Why Most Traders Fail … It's Not What You Think /w Joe Cool Burr

Joe Cool Burr's discussion includes technical analysis, risk, and the discipline involved in trading. The central learning problem is familiar beyond financial markets: how do you distinguish a repeatable process from a few outcomes you liked?

Memory is an unreliable scorekeeper. After an event, an ambiguous signal can look decisive. After a win, a rule may feel more certain than the evidence supports. A written record preserves what you believed before you knew what would happen.

## Make the process inspectable

A useful review distinguishes the conditions you noticed, the action you intended, the boundary you set, and what you actually did. It also records occasions when you chose not to act. Otherwise the record can become a collection of highlights that hides how often the conditions were unclear.

Historical testing raises another question: did you discover a broadly useful pattern, or repeatedly adjust the rules until they fit the examples you already had? New information may behave differently from the data used to develop an idea.

None of this establishes that a strategy will be profitable. It explains why disciplined recordkeeping matters when outcomes contain uncertainty. As you watch, pay attention to the relationship between stated rules and observed behavior. The most revealing gap may be between the process a person describes and the process their own record shows.
