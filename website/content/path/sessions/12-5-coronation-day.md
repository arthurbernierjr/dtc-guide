This is Coronation Day. The Final Boss fight. But here is the secret: the Final Boss is not some algorithm question or trick interview. The Final Boss is you, standing in front of an audience, demoing something real that you built with your own hands. And you are going to win. Not because it is easy. Because you are ready. Every skill, every late night, every debugging session led here. Welcome to the arena. Let's do this.

### What the Run button does

The Run button on this page runs a small model in your browser. It does not install a language, open a database, start a server, charge a card, call an AI provider, or move money. The longer walkthroughs are files and commands for a project on your own machine. Install those tools yourself from the official docs linked on this page, keep secrets in your own environment, and use made-up data in the exercises.

A green checkpoint means this page's model behaved the way the checkpoint claims. It does not mean a service you have not started is working. If a tool is missing, keep designing, write down the result you expect, and mark that check as not run. A screenshot of a result you did not produce is not a result.

### Demonstrate the work you can support

Begin with the user problem, show the main journey, and explain one decision that improved the result. Use the tested revision and synthetic fixtures. If a service fails, acknowledge the failure and use the labeled fallback. A calm, accurate explanation is stronger than pretending an error did not occur. The demonstration is a communication exercise as well as a product exercise.

State what has been verified and what remains limited. Local tests, sandbox payments, fixture-based AI responses, and a deployed smoke test are different evidence. Do not collapse them into a general claim that everything is production ready. A capstone can be impressive while still having a small user base, manual operations, or unfinished optional features. Precision makes the accomplishment easier to trust.

### Make the handoff usable without you

The repository should contain an accurate README, manual setup instructions, environment variable names, test commands, and a description of the architecture. Include the release identifier, public URL if available, known issues, and operational procedures. Keep secret values out of the handoff. Another developer should be able to understand the application and identify the next action without replaying your presentation.

Document data ownership, backups, and recovery. If the product will remain live, decide who notices errors, updates dependencies, renews domains, and handles provider bills. If it is only a demonstration, say so and define what will be shut down to avoid unnecessary cost. Launch creates ongoing responsibilities even when the first release is small.

### Turn feedback into decisions

Capture feedback in the speaker's words before translating it into tasks. Separate reproducible defects, unmet needs, and suggested solutions. A request for a button may reveal a deeper navigation problem. Ask follow-up questions about context and frequency. Prioritize using the product goal and evidence rather than implementing every suggestion in the order received.

Choose one near-term experiment. It might test whether users return, whether onboarding is understandable, or whether a manual workflow is worth automating. State the hypothesis, method, observation, and decision rule. Avoid vague plans such as keep improving the app. A small measurable next step preserves the learning rhythm established throughout the course.

### Tell an accurate portfolio story

Explain your role, the problem, constraints, implementation decisions, and results. Credit collaborators and tools appropriately. Describe how you reviewed AI-assisted code and which tests support your claims. Include a before-and-after example or a difficult failure you diagnosed. A portfolio story is more persuasive when it shows judgment and evidence rather than only a list of technologies.

Keep the story proportional to the evidence. A successful classroom demo is not the same as operating at large scale. A few interviews are not a market survey. A sandbox checkout is not revenue. These distinctions do not diminish the work; they help a reader understand exactly what you accomplished and where the next milestone begins.

### Close the course with a deliberate continuation

Review the artifacts from the terminal exercises through the capstone. Identify one skill that now feels dependable, one that still requires reference material, and one you want to practice next. Choose a realistic schedule and a concrete output for that practice. You do not need to master every tool before building useful software, but you do need a way to keep learning and checking your work.

Submit the final repository, demo evidence, handoff packet, retrospective, and next-step plan. This is the end of the curriculum, so there is no hidden next required session. The next quest is yours: operate the product, deepen a technical skill, interview users, or build another small system with better judgment than you had at the start.

## The demonstration

**Live Demo Presentations**

Give the demo once, out loud, to a person who was not in the build with you. Five minutes for the product, then two minutes where they get to ask anything.

**The five minutes**
- Share your screen and walk the product, not the repository
- Name the problem, the person who has it, and the one path you are about to click
- Show the features that path actually needs
- Say the stack in one sentence, when the path is done
- End with what you want them to do next, or what you will build next

**The two minutes**
- Take two or three questions
- Questions about a product decision, a technical scar, the money, or the next month are all fair
- If you do not know, say what you would check. Do not invent a metric

**Then stop**
- Write down the question that surprised you
- That question is the first item in the next session of work, which is now yours

*If someone's live demo breaks:*
- Switch to the Loom backup video
- No penalty, no stress. This happens to professionals too.
- The ability to recover gracefully IS the skill

**Watch for this:**
- Give a 1-minute warning during each demo
- Keep Q&A focused and constructive
- After each demo, offer one specific piece of positive feedback before the next presenter
- Keep energy high throughout. This is a celebration.

Take ten minutes.

## Where it breaks

![](diagram:session-12-5/fig2-diagnosis)

### Launch treated as the end

The app is deployed and you close the laptop, and next week a dependency advisory arrives, a signup mail bounces, and nobody owns either one. Launch is the start of operating the thing. Name who watches it, what "broken" means, and what you will do in the first hour. Write the follow-up you already know you owe. A product without an owner is a demo that happens to have a URL. The coronation is the handoff to yourself, not the last commit.

### Feedback becomes an unranked wish list

You leave with thirty notes and no order, so the loudest one wins. Split them before you thank anyone. A defect is something the path does wrong. A need is something a person could not complete. A suggestion is a taste. Defects and blocked needs outrank suggestions. Pick one, say why, and leave the rest written down so the decision is visible. A list you do not rank is a way to feel busy while the broken step stays broken.

### Portfolio overclaims

The README says you designed, built, and shipped a platform, and the commit history is a tutorial you followed plus a button. Describe what you actually did, what you verified, and what you left unfinished. Link the deploy and the test command. A precise account of a small real thing is stronger than a claim a reviewer can disprove in five minutes, and they will try. The portfolio is a report of the work. It is not a second pitch that the work cannot support.

**The One Habit That Protects Everything: Code. Every. Day.**

Skills you spent 21 weeks building will decay in 18 days of not using them. The single highest-leverage post-program habit is a daily coding streak:

- **Commit something every day for the next 100 days.** A feature, a bug fix, a refactor, a code challenge — size does not matter, the streak does. Your GitHub contribution graph (the green heat map on your profile) becomes both your accountability system and, honestly, a signal that recruiters and investors do look at. A wall of green says "this person ships."
- **Structure the habit:** 30-60 minutes minimum. Product work counts. Code challenges count (one LeetCode/Codewars problem keeps Unit 9 skills warm). Tutorial code counts only if you push it and can explain it.
- **The 100 Days of Code challenge** is a ready-made frame: code daily, log one sentence about what you did, share it publicly. Public commitment is the cheat code for consistency.
- **If freelancing tempts you** (a great way to fund your product while you build): your capstone is your proof of competence. Start with small fixed-scope projects, always use a written agreement, charge for outcomes rather than hours as soon as you can, and never hand over final deliverables before final payment. Every freelance project is also portfolio material and a potential recurring client.

### Review the artifact

Your deliverable is **A final capstone handoff, demo retrospective, and measurable next-step plan**. Keep the result, the evidence, and the limitations together so a reviewer can reproduce the important behavior. Start the review with the user outcome, then trace the three boundaries shown in the working-model diagram. Explain what enters each boundary, what leaves it, and which condition would stop progress.

Use the failure cases above as a rehearsal, not only as reading. Choose a valid fixture, change one relevant condition, and compare the result. Record expected and observed behavior separately. If they disagree, investigate the smallest boundary that could explain the difference. After correcting it, repeat the original valid case to check that the repair did not remove useful behavior.

Ask a peer to review the artifact without your narration. Give them the setup instructions and acceptance criteria, then note where they need additional information. Improve the artifact or its instructions at that point. A complete submission is understandable without the author's memory. Include unresolved questions as explicit follow-up work with a reason and a next action; do not hide them behind a general claim that everything works.

### Judging Notes

**How to judge your own demo:**
- A simple product you can walk with confidence beats a complicated one you cannot explain.
- The live path is the moment that counts. Tests, security, and code quality are what make that path believable.
- If the live demo breaks, switch to the recording and say that you switched. Recovering is part of the demo.
- The questions worth answering are product questions. "Why this feature?" matters more than "which database?"
- Write the feedback down as next work, not as a verdict on the last 21 weeks.

**Peer Feedback Framework:**
- "I liked how you [specific thing]"
- "The [feature/design/flow] was really well done because [reason]"
- "One thing that could make this even better is [specific suggestion]"
- Never: "This is bad" or "You should have done X." Always frame feedback as additive.
